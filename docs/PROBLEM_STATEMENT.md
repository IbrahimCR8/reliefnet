# Problem Statement

## The Coordination Crisis in Disaster Response

Every year, natural disasters — floods, cyclones, wildfires, and infrastructure collapses — displace millions of people and cause devastating loss of life and property. Yet a recurring, preventable failure compounds the damage: **poor coordination between affected communities and the organizations trying to help them.**

---

## 1. Information Silos Between Residents and Relief Organizations

When disaster strikes, information is scattered across WhatsApp groups, social media posts, and phone calls. Residents in affected areas have ground-level knowledge — which roads are flooded, which neighborhoods are cut off, where food supplies have run out — but that information rarely reaches the organizations with the resources to act on it.

Relief organizations, in turn, operate from central offices with incomplete situational awareness. They dispatch teams to locations reported by days-old news coverage, while unreported pockets of need go unaddressed. The result is duplication of effort in some areas and total absence of support in others.

---

## 2. Volunteer Management Is Largely Manual

During major disasters, hundreds of volunteers mobilize quickly. But matching volunteer skills to incident requirements is typically handled through spreadsheets, word of mouth, or informal coordinators managing WhatsApp threads. This approach fails to scale:

- Volunteers show up to locations already covered, wasting travel time
- Volunteers with specialized skills (medical, engineering, heavy vehicle operation) are deployed to tasks that don't use those skills
- There is no reliable record of who is assigned to what, making accountability impossible
- Coordinators are overwhelmed by inbound communication rather than focusing on logistics

---

## 3. No Real-Time Visibility Into Incident Status

Even when incidents are reported, there is typically no feedback loop. Residents who file reports don't know whether anyone received them. Volunteers on the ground have no way to update headquarters on their progress. Organizations have no dashboard showing which incidents are resolved, which are escalating, and which have no resources allocated.

This opacity wastes resources: multiple teams may respond to the same incident, while an evolving crisis nearby goes unnoticed.

---

## 4. The Gap Between Emergency Calls and Sustained Relief

Emergency phone lines (112, fire brigade, coast guard) are optimized for immediate life-threatening situations — "my house is on fire," "there is a body in the water." They are not designed for the slow-burn, multi-day coordination required by sustained relief operations:

- "We have had no drinking water for 48 hours and the road is still blocked"
- "There are 30 elderly residents in the community hall with no medical supplies"
- "The bridge is damaged; vehicles cannot pass"

These situations don't belong in emergency queues, but they are equally urgent. There is no structured, accessible channel for reporting and tracking them.

---

## 5. Trust and Accessibility Barriers

Requiring residents to create accounts, provide ID, or call government hotlines creates friction precisely when people are stressed, wet, and dealing with damaged phones and intermittent connectivity. Fear of bureaucracy, distrust of institutions, or simply lacking the cognitive bandwidth to register an account means many incidents go unreported.

---

## The Opportunity

A lightweight, accessible, privacy-respecting coordination platform — one that works on mobile, requires no account to file a report, and gives coordinators a structured queue of verified incidents — could meaningfully reduce the coordination overhead of disaster response and direct resources where they are most needed.

ReliefNet is our attempt to build that platform.
